**Monthly dividend stocks** pay on a monthly schedule, but payment frequency does not make a company safer. In 2026, Realty Income (O) reported a $0.2705 monthly dividend in its March 31 filing, while Main Street Capital (MAIN) reported regular monthly dividends of $0.265 per share for each month of the third quarter. The useful comparison is the cash-flow source, balance sheet, payout policy, and price—not the word “monthly” alone.

## What counts as a monthly dividend stock?

A monthly dividend stock or fund makes a cash distribution about twelve times a year. The category is concentrated in REITs, business development companies, closed-end funds, and income-oriented ETFs. That concentration matters: a portfolio can look diversified by ticker while still depending on the same property, credit, interest-rate, or option-income risks.

| Feature | Monthly payer | Quarterly payer |
|---|---|---|
| Cash-flow rhythm | About 12 payments per year | About 4 payments per year |
| Main advantage | Smoother budgeting | Larger and often simpler payment cycle |
| Main trap | Chasing a high headline yield | Assuming quarterly means safer |
| What to research | Distribution source and coverage | Earnings, payout and growth |

## Two different monthly-income models

### Realty Income (O): property rent and a long payment record

Realty Income is a net-lease REIT. In its March 31, 2026 filing, the company reported a monthly dividend of $0.2705 per share, or $3.246 annualized, and described a 57-year history of paying monthly dividends. The filing used a March 31 share price of $61.18 for its yield calculation, which produced about 5.3% at that date.

That figure is historical and price-dependent. A lower share price can push the displayed yield up even when the monthly cash amount has not changed. Read O's [company filing](https://www.realtyincome.com/sites/realty-income/files/realty-income/press-releases/O-03-31-26-10Q-Document-Final.pdf) for the reporting date and assumptions.

### Main Street Capital (MAIN): a business development company

MAIN lends to and invests in lower-middle-market companies. Its investor-relations page reported regular monthly dividends of $0.265 per share for July, August, and September 2026, or $0.795 for the quarter. It also listed a $0.30 supplemental dividend payable in September 2026.

The supplemental payment is exactly why a simple annualisation can mislead. Regular monthly dividends and supplemental dividends are different lines of the policy. MAIN's [dividend information page](https://www.mainstcapital.com/investors/listed-securities-information/dividends) should be checked before using any current figure in a calculation.

## A practical screen for monthly dividend stocks

| Screen | Questions to ask | Warning sign |
|---|---|---|
| Distribution source | Rent, net investment income, option premium, or return of capital? | The label “dividend” hides different economics |
| Coverage | Does recurring cash flow cover the regular distribution? | Coverage depends on asset sales or leverage |
| Balance sheet | When do debt maturities arrive and what are borrowing costs? | Heavy refinancing needs |
| History | Has the regular payment held up across weak markets? | Frequent cuts or unexplained specials |
| Concentration | Is the portfolio tied to one sector or strategy? | Many tickers with the same risk factor |
| Total return | Does the share price keep eroding while cash is paid? | High income paired with long-term capital loss |

This is also why the [high-dividend stock topic](/topics/high-yield-dividend-stocks/) should be read alongside the [dividend stock screening method](/topics/dividend-stock-screening/). Yield is an output of price and cash distribution; it is not a quality score.

## How much capital would a monthly income target require?

Here is a deliberately simple illustration. If a portfolio produced a 5% annual cash yield, before tax and price changes, the capital required for different monthly targets would be:

| Monthly cash target | Annual target | Capital at a 5% illustrative yield |
|---:|---:|---:|
| $100 | $1,200 | $24,000 |
| $500 | $6,000 | $120,000 |
| $1,000 | $12,000 | $240,000 |

The formula is annual income ÷ yield. A 5% yield is an assumption for arithmetic, not a forecast or recommendation. Use the site's [Dividend Calculator](/calculator/) to test different yields, contribution schedules, and reinvestment assumptions.

> “I started with $10,000 split between O and MAIN because I wanted a payment every month. The first statement looked satisfying, but I later realised that the cash schedule was the easy part; deciding how much credit and real-estate risk I could tolerate was harder.”

This is an illustrative scenario, not a reader testimonial. The detour is common: monthly cash feels like a paycheck, so investors stop checking whether the underlying asset value and coverage are holding up.

## Risks that a monthly payment can hide

Monthly payers often cluster in higher-yield sectors. REITs can react to property values, occupancy, refinancing costs, and interest rates. BDCs can face borrower stress and changes in net investment income. Covered-call ETFs can distribute option premium or return of capital, which is not the same as a company earning more profit.

The SEC's [ex-dividend date bulletin](https://www.sec.gov/resources-for-investors/investor-alerts-bulletins/investor-bulletins/ex-dividend-dates-when-are-you-entitled-stock-cash-dividend) is useful for the calendar mechanics, but no calendar can answer whether a distribution is sustainable.

## Questions investors ask about monthly dividend stocks

### Are monthly dividend stocks better than quarterly dividend stocks?

Not automatically. Monthly payments can make budgeting easier, but the underlying business, fund strategy, valuation, and distribution coverage matter more than frequency.

### Which stocks pay dividends every month?

Examples include Realty Income (O) and Main Street Capital (MAIN), but the list changes and the payment policy must be checked on each company's investor-relations page.

### Can monthly dividends replace a salary?

Only if the portfolio is large enough and the income remains sustainable after taxes, fees, inflation, and distribution changes. A monthly schedule does not remove market risk.

### Are monthly dividends taxed differently?

Tax treatment depends on the payer, account type, and the classification shown on tax documents. Do not assume every monthly distribution is a qualified dividend; check the relevant tax form and current [IRS dividend guidance](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc404).

### Should beginners buy the highest-yield monthly payer?

No. Start with distribution source, coverage, debt, concentration, and total return. A very high yield can reflect a falling share price or a distribution that may not last.

### How do I build a monthly income calendar?

First choose investments for quality and fit. Then map their declared ex-dates and payable dates. Three quarterly payers with different schedules can also create monthly cash flow without forcing the portfolio into monthly-only sectors.

## What to do next

Monthly dividend stocks can be useful for cash-flow planning, but the payment calendar is only the visible layer. Compare regular and supplemental distributions, read the latest company filing, calculate the capital requirement, and stress-test a dividend cut before you buy.
